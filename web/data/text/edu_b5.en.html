CHAPTER 5 — THE TIDE HAS A SCHEDULE

First third of the day (00:00–08:00 UTC)

ANTI-MYTH

This chapter has a myth with two edges, equally harmful

Edge one: "the market is random, there are no patterns, any hour is the same as any other." This is disproved in five minutes on our own data — intraday volatility seasonality is one of the most robust facts in financial empirics. Andersen and Bollerslev measured it on DM/USD back in 1998: a dip during the Tokyo lunch break, a rise with the European open, a plateau of peak activity, a spike on US data. We recalculated the same thing on gold with our own data — the curve's shape reproduces.

Edge two, where people who've already heard about sessions tend to fall: "the market is a scheduled conspiracy, institutions agreed at 10:00 to take out my stop." That's also wrong, and worse — it's learned helplessness dressed up as esoterica. The truth sits in the middle and is more useful than either extreme: liquidity has a schedule; direction doesn't. The clock tells you how far the market can move and at what price you'll be filled. Which way it goes — the clock never says.

SBF VOICE · Oleg, Analyst

"Colleague, the question I get most is: 'what's the best time to trade?' My answer isn't what people expect. The best time is whenever you can sit calmly in front of the screen without stealing hours from work or family. Everything else is secondary — a tired person at the perfect hour loses more than a rested one at an imperfect hour.

Then the craft begins: every hour has characteristics, and you need to know them for YOUR hour. Is the spread wide? Is there enough movement for your target to cover costs? Am I about to walk into a release I wasn't expecting? You can trade at 3am — it's just a different market, with different rules, and you need to honestly admit that's the one you're trading.

Separately, about 'stop hunting.' I've heard this explanation for every loss for twelve years. Nobody is hunting your stop personally. Your stop simply sits in exactly the same place as ten thousand other people's stops — past a round number, past the edge of the overnight range. And that's the only place on the chart where a large participant can buy or sell a lot, fast. They're not coming after you. They're coming after liquidity. You're just standing in line holding a sign that says 'volume available here.' Being offended by that makes about as much sense as being offended by the tide."

THE PRICE OF NOT KNOWING

The arithmetic of a bad hour

A trader works days and trades late evening and at night — the only free time he has, entirely honest. Instrument: gold.

At night two factors work against him at once, both measurable. First — spread: in a thin book it's wider than during the day (chapter 4, "Asian night" regime); say he pays 1.2 instead of 0.3 — an overpay of 0.9 points per trade. Second, and heavier: range. Tolerable on its own. But add that targets stop being reached in time, and the trader starts shrinking them — while costs stay fixed. Three months in, he concludes "my strategy stopped working." The strategy worked. The schedule didn't.

And the honest other half, without which the first is manipulation: this doesn't mean you can't trade at night. It means trading differently at night: different targets, different instruments (Asian hours belong to the yen, not the euro), different stops, and always — limit orders instead of market orders.

THE PLANET'S CLOCKS

What actually opens

Before talking about "sessions," two things that textbooks constantly blur need separating. An exchange is a specific venue with a schedule: the Tokyo Stock Exchange runs 09:00–11:30 and 12:30–15:30 Tokyo time, and during the break literally nothing happens there. A currency session isn't a venue — it's a shorthand for the hours when a region's business day is open. Forex doesn't open or close; it runs continuously from Sunday evening to Friday evening. WHY SESSIONS EXIST AT ALL

Why the yen is active at night

In Tokyo, that's simply the working day. The Bank of Japan, exporters like Toyota, regional funds — they all settle their currency needs during their own business hours, like any accounting department. USD/JPY and AUD/USD are moved by real payments in these hours, not speculation.

Why gold explodes at 12:30 UTC

The old version of this chapter said "NYSE opens" here — that's wrong. 12:30 UTC is 8:30am in Washington: when the Bureau of Labor Statistics and other agencies publish data. NFP, inflation, retail sales. Gold reacts harder than most because it's pricing two things at once: the expected Fed rate and dollar strength. NYSE opens an hour later, and it shows up in the data too — just weaker.

What large participants do at night

It's disadvantageous for a large participant to execute big volume in a thin book — it moves price against them (chapter 4). So accumulating volume in illiquid hours is inconvenient, not convenient — the "institutions quietly accumulate at night" story contradicts the mechanics. What actually happens: regional payments settle, low-risk-appetite market-making algos run, and a queue of orders waits for the morning's volume.

Rollover and the midnight spread jump

At 5pm New York time, most brokers begin a new trading day: the daily candle closes, swaps are charged, liquidity providers briefly re-quote. A market order sent at that exact minute is the most expensive order of the day. Wednesday-to-Thursday, the swap is tripled.

THREE ENGINES OF THE DAY

The map showed the humps. Now — who makes them. Exactly three engines, each with an owner, an exact time, and, in two cases out of three, a court case.

Engine 1 · Session overlap

The simplest and most boring one: when two continents are at the table at once, there are twice as many participants, the book is denser, and any news gets twice as many interpreters. The London + New York window is {{overlap_local}} Chisinau time. Practical takeaway: in this window your market order costs the least. Not "you should trade here," but "filling here is cheaper" — a crucial difference.

Engine 2 · Scheduled releases

8:30am in Washington. Not "some news will come out eventually," but a specific minute, known a month in advance, at which thousands of algorithms receive one number simultaneously. Andersen and Bollerslev measured this on DM/USD: the employment report release produces an instant volatility spike of roughly 576%, most repricing happens within 10–20 minutes, elevated background persists for hours. That's exactly why the "Release CPI" button in chapter 4 emptied the order book. The full release calendar is in chapter 6.

Engine 3 · Fixings

The most underrated and most interesting. A fixing is the moment the market is required to produce ONE official number, against which contracts worldwide are settled. Four fixings that matter to us:

The Tokyo fix, 09:55 JST (00:55 UTC). The rate Japanese banks settle client payments at. Ito and Yamada (NBER) measured: a strategy of "hold dollars against yen for five minutes before the fix, sell five minutes after" returned 1.8 basis points on average — noticeably above transaction costs.

"Gotobi days." In Japan, payments traditionally fall on days divisible by five: the 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, 25th, 30th. On these days demand for dollars into the fix is higher, and the effect in the data is noticeably stronger — not esoterica, but an entire country's accounting calendar showing up in quotes.

The LBMA Gold auctions, 10:30 and 15:00 London time. The official gold price the world's contracts settle against, administered since 2015 by ICE Benchmark Administration (before that — the famous "gold fix" in the offices of N M Rothschild & Sons, where representatives of five houses agreed a price twice a day since 1919). Jewellers, central banks and ETFs settle contracts against these two numbers.

The WM/Reuters fix, 16:00 London time. The planet's main FX benchmark — index funds revalue portfolios against it, pulling in tens of billions of dollars of orders at that minute.

🔍 INTERESTING MOMENT · How the manipulation was caught, and what was done about it

Before 2013, the WM/Reuters fix was calculated from trades in a one-minute window: 15:59:30–16:00:30. Dealers at major banks — in chat rooms named things like "The Cartel" and "The Bandits' Club" — traded information about client orders ahead of the fix and coordinated execution to push that minute in a chosen direction. The investigation began with reporting in June 2013. On 12 November 2014, the UK's FCA fined five banks £1.1 billion ($1.7 billion); across all jurisdictions, fines in this case exceeded $10 billion across seven banks, and several dealers faced criminal charges.

Starting 15 February 2015, the calculation window was widened from one minute to five — 15:57:30–16:02:30. The logic is simple: a five-minute window is more expensive to move than a one-minute one.

Researchers (NBER) checked whether the reform worked: partially. Volume distributed more evenly within the window, but the characteristic price reversal right after the fix, and elevated volatility around it, didn't go away.

Market manipulation is real — a proven fact with convictions and fines. It just doesn't look like "I got stopped out"; it looks like dealers colluding in a chat room around one specific minute of one specific benchmark. If someone explains your loss as "manipulation," ask for a name, a date, and a minute. A real one has them.

WHERE STOPS LIVE

The real mechanics of "hunting"

Now the most mythologised topic in the course. Let's take it on data, not on feeling — we happen to have the data.

Carol Osler (then at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York) got access to a large dealing bank's internal book of contingent orders: about 9,700 orders between 1 September 1999 and 11 April 2000, three pairs — dollar/yen, euro/dollar, dollar/pound, average order size $5.8 million. No retail trader ever sees data like this; most talk of "where the stops are" is guesswork. Here — a measurement.

Finding 1 — stops cluster PAST round numbers

Stop orders cluster not on round numbers but just past them — and asymmetrically. Buy stops pile up just above a round level: 14.4% end in 01–10 versus only 7.4% in 90–99. Human logic: "I'll put it with some room past the level so it doesn't get hit by accident" — and everyone thinks that.

Finding 2 — take-profits sit ON round numbers

9.3% of take-profits sit on levels ending in 00, versus 4.4% of stops. Also logical: "I'll exit at two thousand five hundred," not "at 2497.30."

Finding 3 — a cascade, not a reversal

When price reaches such a cluster, stops fire en masse, each entering the market as a market order (chapter 4!) — and they eat further into the book, triggering the next stops. Osler calls this a price cascade. The direct consequence: the move doesn't reverse after a level breaks — it accelerates. The exact post-breakout acceleration technical analysis has described for a century without knowing the mechanics.

Notice how three chapters folded into one picture. Chapter 3 told you WHAT to risk. Chapter 4 — that a stop fills in a queue. Chapter 5 — that everyone shares the same queue, in the same place. That's not three topics. It's one topic from three angles.

THE CLOCK-CHANGE TRAP

Why "at 15:30" is a bad way to say it, and "at 12:30 UTC" is a good one

Everything you learned in this chapter shifts twice a year — and not at the same time. The US changes clocks on the second Sunday of March and the first Sunday of November. The EU — the last Sunday of March and the last Sunday of October. Japan never changes clocks. Russia hasn't changed clocks since 2014.

First: in winter, the entire US schedule slides an hour later relative to fixed time zones. Half the tutorials online are written with "summer" numbers and mislead readers for half the year — including, until this edition, our own chapter.

Second: for Chisinau almost nothing changes — and that's good news. Moldova switches to DST with the EU, and the US switches almost in sync, so American events stay at the same local time year-round. The overlap window is {{overlap_md}} in Chisinau, summer and winter alike.

Third, and most important: three weeks a year, everything shifts out of sync. In 2026 that's 8–29 March and 25 October – 1 November. During these days the gap between London and New York changes by an hour, and the usual "news at half past three" is wrong. Every year, someone misses NFP because their alarm was still set to last year's time.

An event's time lives in a calendar, not in your head. A professional doesn't memorise "NFP at 15:30." They check a calendar where the event sits in UTC and renders in their own locale.

CLIFFHANGER → CHAPTER 6

Now you know which hours the market can move — and exactly why. But look at our map again, at the daily peak. It doesn't land on some exchange's opening bell. It lands at 8:30am in Washington — the minute a government agency publishes a single number.

A number that's known in advance: the date, a year ahead; the hour, a month ahead; analysts' forecasts, a week ahead. Only the value itself is unknown. And the moment it's published, it instantly multiplies volatility several times over — measured at roughly 576 percent on the employment report.

Think about what that means. The day's strongest move isn't made by a secret player or a clever scheme — it's made by a press release sitting in a public calendar you can look at right now. Everyone knows when. Nobody knows what. And an entire industry is built on exactly that gap.
