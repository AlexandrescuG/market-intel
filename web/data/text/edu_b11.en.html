ANTI-MYTH

The myth here has two edges, and each one gets in the way differently

First edge: «robots are stealing money from retail traders». Mechanically false for a retail CFD client, and below we explain why. The harm in this myth isn't its inaccuracy — it's that it removes accountability: if a loss is explained by an invisible force, there's nothing to calculate and nothing to change.

Second edge: «high-frequency trading is pure evil and should be banned». Also an oversimplification. A significant share of these participants act as market makers: holding two-sided quotes and earning the spread. There's research showing spreads tighten with their arrival, and there's work on the costs — things like latency arbitrage, where one participant's gain is paid for by a slower one's loss. It's a genuinely contested topic with literature on both sides, and presenting it as settled would be dishonest.

The truth that's actually useful to you: speed matters where there's a public order queue. A retail CFD client doesn't have one. Their costs live somewhere else — in the spread, the commission, the swap, and the execution quality of their own platform. That's more boring than a conspiracy and, unlike one, it can be measured.

SBF VOICE

Robots don't eat your trades — that checks out with arithmetic: spread and commission on real trades almost always outweigh any conceivable effect of microsecond algorithms. Three things are worth checking on a platform: which legal entity actually signs the contract, the spread during your own hours rather than the advertised one, and what happens to execution during a release. Choosing a platform is a matter of arithmetic, not comfort: the cost difference over a long series of trades is comparable to the difference between a profitable strategy and an unprofitable one.

WHY OFF-EXCHANGE TRADING EXISTS

A direct continuation of chapter 7. A large participant needs to fill a size that will move the price against them. There are two solutions: slice it up (chapter 7), or find a counterparty outside the open book.

That's how venues emerge where orders aren't visible before execution. The point isn't secrecy for its own sake — it's that the mere visibility of a large order worsens its own price.

What it gives you

filling a large size without an immediate price impact.

What it costs

some information about supply and demand never makes it into the public price, so the public price reflects reality worse.

It's a tradeoff, not a crime. Regulators cap the share of this kind of trading precisely because they see both sides of it.

A real number, with a source

Off-exchange trading made up roughly 44–48% of total US equity volume in 2024–2025 per FINRA ATS transparency data; counted narrowly as just «dark pools» (excluding market-maker internalization of retail flow), it's roughly 36–39%. The gap between those two figures is wider than the often-cited «35–40%» range, and that's not a typo — it's a consequence of the terms meaning different things.

HIGH-FREQUENCY TRADING: THREE DIFFERENT THINGS

⚡ Market-making at speed

Holds two-sided quotes, earns the spread, updates faster than humans can. Legal — it's a job. The practical consequence for everyone: tighter spreads in calm times, and those spreads vanishing at moments of uncertainty (chapter 4 and chapter 6: orders pull back ahead of a release).

🏁 Latency arbitrage

Exploiting the fact that the same piece of information doesn't reach different venues at the same instant. Legal, but contested: the gain here is paid for by a slower participant's loss. This is exactly what the literature argues about, and exactly why cables get built and servers get placed next to exchanges.

🚫 Front-running

Executing your own trade ahead of a client order you know about because of your position. A violation. It has nothing to do with high-frequency trading as such — it's about abusing access, not about speed.

The first two are ways of doing business; the third is a violation. Lumping them under one word is convenient for drama and harmful for understanding. The previous edition of this chapter had its own interactive called the «Front-Running Simulator», showing the reader's own order getting intercepted. We rewrote it: first, because that's a claim about unlawful conduct we never proved; second, because this mechanism doesn't apply to a retail order at all — we explain why right now.

Estimates of high-frequency trading's share of US equity market volume vary between researchers, but converge around 50% in the mid-2020s — up from about 26% in 2006 and about 61% in 2009 (a post-crisis peak; estimates have fluctuated since). The spread reflects different definitions of a «high-frequency» participant, not a measurement error.

WHERE YOU ARE IN THIS PICTURE

Everything described above happens where there's a public order queue: stock, futures, and options exchanges. Speed matters because there's a queue you can get ahead of.

Now look at your own trade. You trade CFDs or spot forex at a platform. Your order never enters any exchange queue at all: it's filled internally — against the platform's own book, or at its liquidity provider. There's no public queue anywhere along that path.

A simple thing follows from this: a high-frequency participant in New Jersey physically cannot see your order and can't do anything with it. Not because you're too small — because the two of you are in different systems entirely.

That's bad news for the mythology and good news for you. Bad, because it removes a convenient explanation for losses. Good, because the real intermediary in your chain is exactly one, it's known by name, its terms are published, and it can be chosen. There was nothing you could do about the algorithms in New Jersey.

A-BOOK, B-BOOK, AND THE CONFLICT OF INTEREST

A platform can handle your trade in one of two ways.

Route it to an external liquidity provider (A-book)

Then it earns from commission and a markup on the spread, and its income doesn't depend on whether you won or lost.

Keep it internally (B-book)

Then it's the other side of your trade. Your loss becomes its income; your profit becomes its expense.

The second option is often described as a scam. It isn't: a significant share of retail flow is processed internally at most platforms worldwide, because routing every small trade externally costs more than netting them against each other. The model is legal and disclosed in the documents. The problem isn't that it exists — it's the conflict of interest it creates, which is worth the client understanding.

WHAT TO MEASURE YOURSELF

Fifteen minutes in your terminal, and you'll know more about your own platform than any review can tell you.

1 · The spread during your hours

Not the advertised one, not the session-overlap one — the one during the hours you actually trade (chapter 5, your window). Write down three readings on different days.

2 · The spread at a release

A minute before a calendar publication and a minute after (chapter 6). The difference is what you're paying to enter at that moment.

3 · Slippage

Compare the price in your order to the fill price across a few market orders. You can see this on a demo too.

4 · Swap

What it costs to carry a position overnight, and over a Wednesday rollover. Often turns out to be the main cost for anyone holding positions for a while.

Write it in your journal. In chapter 14 we'll work out what this costs across a series of trades, and we'll need your numbers there, not ours.
