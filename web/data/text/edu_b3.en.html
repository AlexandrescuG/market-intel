ANTI-MYTH

“Diversification means buying more different things”

No. Buying the S&P 500, NASDAQ, Apple, and Bitcoin is buying the same risk four times over: they all fall together when money gets expensive (chapter 2) or fear arrives. Real diversification means assets that fall for DIFFERENT reasons. There are fewer of those than you'd think, and right when you need it most, correlations converge to one — “in a crisis, everything falls except the cause of the crisis.” That's why for a professional, diversification is the third line of defense. The first two are position size and the stop.

THE ARSENAL

Six Characters

Before building a portfolio, you need to understand the instruments. Tap a card.

Stocks

The growth engine — a stake in a business, works as long as the economy grows.

Corporate profits and the Fed rate (expensive money discounts future profits — 2022 showed exactly how).

Against inflation over the long run (businesses pass prices on to buyers).

First in any crisis — both fear (2008, 2020) and inflation (2022). Drawdowns of −50% or deeper are a normal part of its biography.

Bonds

A creditor's instrument — you lend to a government/company at a fixed rate.

Directly by the rate: rates rise → old low-coupon bonds become unwanted → price falls.

In crises of FEAR (2008: +2.2% over 17 months, with a mid-panic dip to −11%; 2020: +3.5% over the window — flight to quality).

In crises of INFLATION — 2022, −14.4% for the year, the worst year in nearly a century. The entire 60/40 lesson comes from here: bonds protect against panic, not against money getting more expensive.

Gold

The oldest money — produces nothing, owes nothing to anyone, so it can't go bankrupt.

The real rate (rate minus inflation) and fear.

Against inflation and catastrophe; in 2022 it ended the year at +1.1% in dollar terms — “zero” when everything else was −14…−34%.

In eras of high real rates it can go sideways for years. And even as a “safe haven” it isn't perfect: in March 2020 it fell alongside everything else, −6% in a week of panic — liquidity was raised from anything sellable — before bouncing back to close the year at +25%.

Oil

The economy's bloodstream, the most volatile asset in the arsenal — from −70% to hundreds of percent within months.

Global economic demand, OPEC, and geopolitics; its link to the rate is crooked — that's its value.

In inflationary crises it's often the cause of the inflation — 2022 ended in the green, +5.5%.

In demand crises: in 2020 the May WTI futures contract went NEGATIVE, −$37.63 — the price really was negative (we unpack this in the “How the price became −$37” scene).

Crypto

The youngest, most money-sensitive asset — “stocks cubed.”

Liquidity (chapter 2): turn on QE → crypto flies first; raise rates → it falls deeper than anything: 2022 = −64…−74%.

It has never once protected in a dollar crisis — it fell together with NASDAQ, just harder.

Without a stop — forever (see “The Price of Not Knowing”). In a professional's portfolio, size it as “money I can lose entirely.”

Cash

The boring champion of every crisis — the only asset with no drawdown.

Directly by the rate — in 2022, dollar cash started paying for the first time in 15 years (5%+ by 2023).

Always against volatility; during inflation it melts itself, but its EXCHANGE RATE against other currencies can rise (DXY +7.6% in 2022).

Over the long run — the only asset with guaranteed negative real returns. Cash isn't an investment; cash is ammunition.

Notice the pattern: every character is described through the rate from chapter 2. One organization changes one number — and all six assets react, each in its own way. Now, into the lab: let's test it.

THE MATH OF SURVIVAL

The Math of Survival

Drawdown Asymmetry

Losses and recovery aren't symmetric: −10% is cured by +11% growth, but −50% requires +100%, and −90% requires +900%. This isn't an opinion — it's arithmetic.

At what percentage of drawdown does an account become practically unrecoverable for a beginner? Drag the slider and decide for yourself.

Pros keep account drawdown within 10–20% — not out of modesty, but out of arithmetic.

Drag the slider to −96% (the real SOL case from “The Price of Not Knowing”) to see +2400%.

1% risk isn't about cowardice. It's about the fact that 5 losses in a row is normal weather for any strategy (at a 50% win rate, a streak of 5 is nearly certain over a hundred trades), and your deposit has to survive it without even noticing.

EMOTIONAL TRAINER

Survive the Streak

Twenty trades, a fixed random sequence, RR 1:2. You choose the risk per trade before starting. After every three losses in a row, the game pauses and asks what you do next. A second line always runs alongside yours — the same risk you picked at the start, unchanged for the rest of the run.

At this risk, five losses in a row cost {{dd}}% of the account — recovering from that needs a {{rec}}% gain.

Stopped for today — your line is frozen for the rest of the run.

It was the same sequence of trades — fixed, identical for everyone. Only your sizing decisions diverged. The disciplined line reached {{base}}, yours reached {{user}}. The strategy has nothing to do with it — it carried both lines the same way.

This is a model on a fixed random sequence, not a promise of returns.

TOOLS

A stop is the pre-paid cost of the information that you were wrong. Expensive? Compare it to the cost of finding that out without a stop — see the section above.

A stop protects against price MOVEMENT. Against the price VANISHING (a gap — chapter 2's “the franc snaps its peg” station) only position size protects you. And there's a third threat the textbooks stay quiet about: your stop can be filled hundreds of points worse than its level ON AN ORDINARY DAY. Who does that, and why — next chapter opens up the exchange's engine room.

The one formula in this course you need to know by heart: Size = (Deposit × Risk%) ÷ Distance to stop. The calculator will figure out everything else.

The price is pulled live from the terminal; the stop sits beyond the nearest real level.

April 17, 2020. The May WTI futures contract expires April 21. Holders must either sell it or accept physical delivery of oil at storage tanks in Cushing, Oklahoma — and storage is almost full.

April 20. Oil demand had collapsed (lockdowns), and there's nowhere left to physically store it. A day before expiry, traders still holding contracts are willing to PAY just to avoid having oil delivered they have nowhere to store. The price goes negative: closes at −$37.63, intraday as low as −$40.32.

April 21, the last day of trading for May. The contract opens negative again (−$14) and falls to −$16.74, before closing at +$10.01 as trading shifts to the June contract, which never went negative.

“Buying oil” on an exchange almost always means buying a futures contract or a CFD — a settlement instrument tied to the delivery price, not the delivery itself. Retail instruments (ETFs, broker CFDs) didn't go negative that day — brokers roll positions to the next contract ahead of time. It was specifically the physically-deliverable expiring contract that went negative.
