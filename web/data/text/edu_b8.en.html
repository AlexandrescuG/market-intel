ANTI-MYTH

«Correct settings exist, and professionals know them»

Out of this grow the «golden» combinations: 9/21 for scalping, 50/200 for an investor, 20 for everything else. The numbers get passed around as knowledge, even though none of them were derived — they stuck historically because they mapped conveniently onto the calendar: 20 trading days in a month, 50 a quarter, 200 a year.

This gets tested in a single screen, and we let you test it yourself (below). The result is known in advance, and it isn't in our favor as a seller: the best period over any stretch of history always exists, and on the next stretch it turns out not to be the best one. Not «sometimes» — systematically, and further down we show by how much.

The opposite edge of the myth is just as harmful: «indicators don't work, it's all drawing». That's wrong too. A moving average honestly does exactly what it claims: it averages. The problem isn't the tool — it's that people expect a prediction from it that its formula doesn't contain.

SBF VOICE

We keep a single moving average on the chart. It answers exactly one question: is price above or below its average over the chosen horizon. The period isn't picked by optimization — it's set by how long a typical trade lasts. Parameters fitted to history stop working right around when you start trusting them.

THE PRICE OF NOT KNOWING

The arithmetic of someone else's setup

Someone takes a ready-made combination from a video — say, EMA 8 and 20 — and trades the crossovers on the hourly chart. Over a month, dozens of crossovers pile up: fast averages on the hourly cross often, and that's a property of the setting, not the market.

At {{crosses_per_month}} crossovers a month (our measurement for this pair of periods on this timeframe), they pay {{monthly_cost}} points a month just for the right to follow the signals, before any conversation about whether they're profitable at all.

What follows is predictable: a run of false crossovers in a range-bound market, the person decides the settings are «too fast», switches to 21/50, runs into a different market regime, switches back. Every switch is a fit to the last month — the same mistake as in the lab below, just stretched over time and paid for in real money.

The honest second half: the issue isn't that 8/20 is a bad combination. It's that it's someone else's. The number of crossovers, the cost per trade, and whether you live to see the average turn all depend on the instrument and your own horizon — and all of that can be worked out in fifteen minutes on your own chart. That's exactly what we'll ask you to do at the end of the chapter.

WHAT A MOVING AVERAGE ACTUALLY IS

k = 2 / (period + 1) — the weight with which a new bar enters the average. The shorter the period, the larger k, the faster the EMA reacts to a fresh price.

The average contains no information that isn't already in the price

It's a transformation of already-known numbers: added, weighted, divided. No new information appears in the result — what appears is ease of reading. Everything the indicator «shows» was already on the chart; it just removed some of the detail so the rest stands out.

A rule for every chapter that follows

An indicator can't know more than the price it's calculated from. If someone claims otherwise, they aren't talking about arithmetic.

LAG ISN'T A FLAW — IT'S A PRICE

Any smoothing pays for itself with a delay. The longer the period, the fewer false wiggles, and the later the average reacts to a genuine reversal. This isn't «a shortcoming of EMA, fixed by other indicators» — it's a property of the averaging operation itself, and it can't be worked around, only positioned on a scale.

Where you land on this scale depends on how long you hold a position — and on nothing else.

WHAT A MOVING AVERAGE IS ACTUALLY GOOD FOR

📐 A definition, not a forecast

«Uptrend» is vague and shifts with mood. «Price above its N-period average» is arithmetic, the same yesterday and today. The average's value is that it fixes a definition and doesn't let you argue with yourself after the fact. That's discipline, not prediction.

🔍 Describing the regime

The price's distance from the average and the average's slope describe what state the instrument is in right now: has it drifted far, or is it oscillating around it. That's context for everything else — including what the following chapters teach. A description of the present, not the future.

🗣 A shared language

When two people say «price is below the two-hundred», they understand each other exactly. That's the only reason it's worth knowing about 50 and 200 — not because the numbers are special, but because plenty of people watch them, and that makes conversation possible.

CLIFFHANGER → CHAPTER 9

You've learned not to trust settings picked off the past. What's left is learning not to trust yourself.

The next episode is about company earnings and oscillators. And there's a trap there that catches even people who've been through this chapter in full.

Open any company's chart after an earnings release, and the picture will look obvious: here they expected weak numbers, here strong ones came out, here's where price went. You'll look at it and decide you understood it. The problem is that once the outcome is already known, memory rebuilds itself as if you'd expected exactly that all along. That isn't a character flaw — it's how memory works, and it wrecks most people's independent attempts to learn from history.

There's exactly one way to catch yourself doing it, and it's an unpleasant one: write down what you expect before you find out. The next chapter is built around exactly that — and along the way, explains why a stock falls on an earnings report that beat forecast.
