ANTI-MYTH

«A good report means the stock rises. A bad one, it falls.»

Disproved by the same mechanism as chapter 6: what trades isn't the level, it's the distance to expectation. But here a second layer gets added that doesn't exist in macro data: a company's report contains not just the past, but the future. Guidance — the company's own forecast for the next quarter — often weighs more than the fact for the quarter just closed, because the fact is already half-known to the market from related data, while guidance isn't.

The opposite edge: «earnings are a lottery, you can't trade through them». That's wrong too, but differently: it isn't a lottery — it's an event with a known date and an unknown outcome, exactly what chapter 6 taught. The rule is the same: the calendar tells you when and roughly how much, but never which way.

SBF VOICE

In hindsight, every earnings report and every divergence looks predictable: we look at the reversal and find the signal that "explained" it. There's one real test — write the call down before the event, not after. Without a recorded forecast, accuracy is indistinguishable from chance, while hindsight confidence is almost always near-perfect — and those are two different numbers.

THE PRICE OF NOT KNOWING

Analysis without feedback

Someone studies earnings reports on historical charts. They open up ten cases, see an «obvious» picture in each one, derive a rule, and start trading. The very first real reports go against the rule, and they conclude that «the market changed» or that they «need more experience».

Let's work out what this actually costs, without guessing at the outcome: the cost isn't in losses on individual trades — it's in lost learning time. Someone studying the market in hindsight gets no feedback at all — every retrospective confirms they were right. They can spend a whole year like this and get nowhere, because the learning mechanism never engages: there's no moment where reality pushes back.

The honest second half: studying history is useful — but only as a way to understand the mechanics, not as a way to measure yourself. You can only measure yourself going forward, and it costs one line, written down before the event.

HOW TO READ AN EARNINGS REPORT

Revenue

Total sales for the period. What matters isn't the absolute number but year-over-year growth and how it stacks up against analyst consensus.

EPS (earnings per share)

Net income divided by share count. Often published in two forms — GAAP (standard accounting) and adjusted — and the market usually trades the adjusted figure against consensus.

Operating margin

The share of revenue left after operating expenses. Shows whether producing the same volume of sales is getting more or less expensive for the company.

Segmentation

The breakdown of revenue/profit by business line or region. This, not the headline number, is often where the reason for a move actually sits — say, one segment slowing while total revenue grows.

Guidance

The company's own forecast for the next period. The only forward-looking part of the report — and often the most important one for price.

What the market already knows before publication

A report is rarely a complete surprise: some of the data leaks out through industry statistics, supplier and customer reports, and preliminary estimates. By the time of publication, analyst consensus isn't a guess — it's an aggregated estimate from people who follow the company professionally. That's why the surprise is usually small, and what actually drives the move is the detail: guidance, segment margin, management's comments on the call.

WHY STOCKS FALL ON A GOOD REPORT

The expectation is already in the price

The same mechanism as chapter 6. If the market spent a week pricing in a strong report, the buyers ran out before publication.

Guidance matters more than the fact

The fact is the past, guidance is the future, and the company gets valued on the future. Hence the «beat every line and still fell» configuration: the past confirmed expectations, the future undercut them.

A reaction to the reaction

The first move often reverses over the course of the day: orders fire, positions built ahead of the event get closed out, people who were waiting for a price step in. The first candle after publication isn't the market's conclusion — it's its first reply.

What we're NOT claiming. That guidance «matters more» systematically. The three cases below show three different mechanisms; how often each one wins — we haven't measured, and until we have, we won't say.

OSCILLATORS: WHAT THEY ACTUALLY MEASURE

RSI

RSI compares the average size of up-moves to the average size of down-moves over the last N bars. That's it. A value of 70 means exactly one thing: the recent rise has been fast relative to recent declines. It doesn't mean «expensive», and it doesn't mean «due for a drop».

The word «overbought» is misleading here, and that's not a nitpick about terminology: in a strong trend, RSI sits above 70 for weeks, and the whole time it's formally pointing at a reversal that isn't happening. We measured what actually happens after RSI > 70 — below.

MACD

MACD is the difference between two exponential moving averages — exactly the object you worked with last chapter. Everything said there about fitted parameters applies here too: 12, 26, and 9 aren't derived constants, they're numbers that stuck historically. A line crossover is a statement that the short average has overtaken the long one. Not a prediction.

Divergence

Divergence is a mismatch between a new price extreme and the absence of one on the oscillator. That's a description of a fact: the momentum of the latest move is weaker than the previous one. What comes next is interpretation, and that's where you need to be careful: the exact same mismatch also shows up in the middle of a strong trend, where nothing reverses at all.

And the key part — divergences are mostly visible in hindsight. On a finished chart they're easy to find, because the eye looks for them right next to a reversal that already happened. That's exactly the effect you just measured on yourself.

A method fixed before the count

For RSI: every case of crossing the 70 threshold from below; what price did 5, 10, and 20 bars later; the share of cases where the move went against the prior trend — a «reversal». THREE CASES — THREE MECHANISMS

Three cases are three cases. We showed them so you'd see there are several mechanisms and they're different. How often each one wins — we haven't counted, and so we don't claim. If we start counting, we'll publish it, like everything else in this course.

Which way do you think the stock moved after this report?

When guidance and a leading metric decide it

February 2022. Meta reported roughly in line with expectations but showed the first-ever quarterly decline in daily active users in the company's history — and gave next-quarter guidance below what the market was pricing in.

Reaction: −26.4% in a single session, the largest one-day market-cap loss in US market history at the time. No single fact on its own was a catastrophe — the coincidence of weak guidance and a weak leading metric mattered more than either one alone.

When neither the fact nor guidance decides it — management's commentary does

April 2024. Tesla missed on both earnings per share and revenue — the year-over-year revenue decline was the largest since 2012. By the logic of the first two mechanisms, the stock should have fallen.

It rose: on the investor call, Musk announced an earlier-than-expected launch of affordable models. Neither the fact nor formal guidance changed — a verbal statement decided it.

When the growth rate of a key metric decides it, not the fact itself

January 2026. Microsoft beat consensus on both earnings and revenue.

The stock fell 7%. The reason: growth in the Azure cloud business slowed slightly from the prior quarter against a backdrop of sharply rising AI infrastructure capital spending. The company didn't miss a single published number — the market reacted to the deceleration in growth rate and to what rising spending means for future quarters' margins.
