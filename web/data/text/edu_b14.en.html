ANTI-MYTH

"Short moves are more predictable than long ones"

Wrong, and the opposite is true: at minute scales, the share of noise in price is at its maximum, and the share of structure is at its minimum. That's exactly why a short horizon doesn't make forecasting easier — it makes it harder.

Scalping does give you something else, and it's valuable: many attempts. What a swing trader learns about themselves in a year, a scalper learns in a month — chapter 12 explained why the number of trades matters more than their quality when the question is self-knowledge.

The flip side of the myth: "scalping is a casino, only beginners do it." Also wrong. It's a demanding style with unforgiving arithmetic; the problem isn't the style, it's that it's sold as the most accessible entry point when, by cost and discipline requirements, it's the hardest.

SBF VOICE

Scalping doesn't forgive arithmetic: at a five-point target, spread isn't overhead — it's your main opponent. What works is three steps: measure your own spread in your own hours (chapter 11), compute the breakeven threshold before your first trade, compare it to the real percentage from your fifty demo trades (chapter 12). If the second number is smaller than the first, no amount of discipline fixes that.

It works. Three things you need to know before, not after

First. The starting amount. Not "how much do I need to trade properly," but how much you won't miss.
