ANTI-MYTH

"I'll know if a system works from the first few trades"

You won't, and it's not about paying closer attention. A short run tells you almost nothing but noise: the spread of outcomes over ten trades is so much wider than the gap between a good system and a bad one that they're indistinguishable even in principle. This isn't an opinion — it's computable, and we compute it below.

The opposite edge of the myth is just as harmful: "so testing is pointless, you just have to believe in the system." Also wrong. You can test — the object of the test is just different. Fifty trades can't prove an edge, but they can precisely establish whether you followed your own rules. And unlike an edge, that's entirely in your hands.

The truth is in between: discipline gets tested first, an edge second. It doesn't work in reverse — measuring a system that was executed haphazardly measures nothing about the system itself.

SBF VOICE

Ichimoku isn't better or worse than other ways of describing the market — its strength is being one complete language for entries, exits, and context, instead of a pile of five different indicators. What works with any system, including this one, is the same thing: three rules written down before you start, and fifty trades without changing them. The value of the first fifty isn't the result — it's finding out how many of them actually broke a rule you thought you were following.

THE PRICE OF NOT KNOWING

What ten trades actually prove

A trader tests a system on ten trades. Six wins, four losses. Conclusion: "it works, 60%." Size goes up.

Result "6 of 10 or better" for a system with NO edge (p=50%)

In other words, this result doesn't distinguish a system with an edge from one without: it's equally likely for both.

The cost of the mistake isn't in these ten trades — it's in what happens after: size was increased on a conclusion that showed nothing. A normal losing streak follows, and it arrives on the increased size.

The honest other half: this doesn't mean testing is useless. It means a test result needs a sample-size figure attached, and sizing decisions shouldn't be made on the result of a short run. How many you actually need — we compute below, and it's also why the next chapter asks for a series rather than "give it a try."

WHERE THE SYSTEM CAME FROM

Almost a century old, and one question without a solid answer

The system was created by a Japanese journalist who published under the pseudonym Ichimoku Sanjin, and released in finished form in the mid-twentieth century after decades of testing. The name translates roughly as "balance seen at a glance" — an accurate description of the intent: not a set of signals, one picture.

FIVE LINES

What each line actually measures

The formulas and the visual logic haven't changed from the previous edition — what changed is what we claim about each line.

Tenkan-Sen

Analogous to a fast moving average but computed differently: not the average of closes, but the midpoint between the high and low over 9 periods. Shows the midpoint of the last 9 bars' range, not the average price.

A flat Tenkan means sideways movement; a steep one means strong momentum. The angle is more informative than the value itself. The difference from an MA is substantive: it averages the extremes of the range, not the closes.

Kijun-Sen

The system's main 'balance line': the midpoint of the range over 26 periods. Often acts as a dynamic level price returns to after impulsive moves.

Kijun's angle is a rough indicator of trend quality: a flat Kijun inside the Cloud often coincides with a zone where price lingers longer. The formula reflects the range midpoint, not the average closing price — the same distinction as Tenkan.

Chikou Span

The most unusual element: today's closing price plotted 26 periods in the past. A convenient way to see at a glance where we are relative to a month ago.

The common rule — 'Chikou above price 26 periods ago = signal confirmed' — is a tautology, unpacked separately below. As a standalone way to see how far price has moved over a period, it's useful. As independent confirmation, it isn't: it's the same observation stated a second time.

Senkou Span A

The fast edge of the Kumo Cloud: the average of Tenkan and Kijun, plotted 26 periods forward. Why the forward shift doesn't grant knowledge of the future — unpacked separately below, the chapter's key explanation.

In a bull trend, pullbacks often stop at Senkou A. A thin Cloud (A≈B) means a weaker trend, a breakout is more likely. Thickness describes the spread of range-midpoint estimates across different periods, not the strength of a future move.

Senkou Span B

The slow Cloud edge: the range midpoint over 52 periods, also plotted 26 periods forward. Together with Senkou A it forms the Cloud.

A flat Senkou B often coincides with a zone where price lingered for a long time in the past. A future crossover of Senkou A and B ('Kumo Twist') is a crossover of two averages computed today, not a forecast of a reversal moment.

WHAT'S ACTUALLY VALUABLE IN ICHIMOKU

Coherence

Every element is computed from the same data and speaks the same language. This protects against a common failure mode: stacking five indicators, getting five contradictory opinions, and picking the one that agrees with the position you already opened.

A single version of events

As long as the rules stay fixed, the system gives one answer to 'what state are we in.' It's harder to argue with after the fact than a pile of separate tools — and arguing with yourself after the fact is the main source of losses (chapter 10).

Cloud thickness as description

The distance between the edges describes how far the range-midpoint estimates diverged across different periods. As context for position size — useful. As a prediction — no.

Three conclusions, all three unpleasant

First: proving an edge statistically takes hundreds of trades. For most people that's years. This doesn't mean you can't trade earlier — it means confidence in a system based on the first hundred trades rests on nothing, for anyone, including us.

Second: over fifty trades, even a working system's result lands in a wide interval — inside it sit both an "excellent result" and "the system doesn't work." Both conclusions would be equally baseless.

Third: a streak of five losses in a row is a routine event for a working system. If you quit on it, you're not quitting a bad system — you'd quit any system.

Which is the whole point of this arithmetic: the decision to continue or stop can't be made on the result. It's made on whether you followed the rules. The result will come later, and it will be trustworthy later.

This is a sample-size calculator, not a result forecast. We don't publish win rates, ours or anyone else's. All parameters are entered by the user, every output is about sample size.

Test power is set at 80% (the standard value in statistics — the spec doesn't name a number explicitly) — this is an assumption, not a measurement.
