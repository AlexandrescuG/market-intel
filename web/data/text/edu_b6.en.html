THE PRICE OF NOT KNOWING

The arithmetic of a missed release

A trader holds a gold position with a 15-point stop — a normal stop for their timeframe, sized per chapter 3. They didn't check the calendar. A high-impact release hits.

Two factors land at once, and both are measurable. First: the median bar size at the moment of such a release, per our data, is {{release_median_move}} of the daily range — noticeably more than their stop. Second, and this is what decides it: the spread widens at the moment of publication — the market maker pulled its orders (chapter 4). The stop doesn't fill at the stop price, it fills at the first available one.

Outcome: the position closes not because the trader's analysis was wrong, but because they happened to be in the market at the exact moment the book was empty. Forty minutes later, price is back where it was. They conclude «my stop was too tight» and next time set it wider — increasing risk while solving the wrong problem.

The honest second half: this doesn't mean you can never hold a position through a release. It means the decision to hold must be a conscious one: either the position closes before the release, or its size is reduced ahead of time, or the stop is set knowing that in the first minute it guarantees nothing. All three are decisions. Not knowing about the release isn't one.

WHAT AN ECONOMIC CALENDAR ACTUALLY IS

The word «news» is misleading. There's no news in the calendar in the journalistic sense — it's a publication schedule for statistical agencies and central banks. Every row has an owner, an exact minute, and three numbers.

What it was last time. The baseline for comparison.

The median or consensus of an analyst poll. Not one person's opinion and not a guess — it's what's already priced in by the time of publication.

Surprise = Actual − Forecast. That's what moves the market — not the actual value on its own. 180,000 jobs is a great number against a forecast of 120,000 and a bad one against a forecast of 250,000.

Impact

Calendars flag events across three tiers. The flag is useful, but it isn't a law: impact reflects the average historical response, not a guarantee. We have our own measurements (§3.4), and they show the spread within one impact tier is wider than the gap between tiers.

Revisions

One thing rarely written about: many indicators get revised after the fact. An NFP release contains not just the new number but a correction to the prior two months. A strong surprise accompanied by a large negative revision to last month is often read by the market as weak — which explains some of the «confusing» reactions.

SURPRISE, NOT THE NUMBER

Why surprises need to be normalized

The problem: surprises from different releases aren't directly comparable. Missing NFP by 50,000 and missing CPI by 0.2 percentage points are different units at different scales. The fix is normalization: divide the surprise by that indicator's historical standard deviation of surprises. That gives a dimensionless number — «missed by two standard deviations». That's how any two releases get compared against each other.

WHY THE MARKET FALLS ON GOOD NEWS

The most common beginner question, and the simplest answer once you strip out the mysticism.

Mechanism one — the expectation is already in the price

The forecast is known in advance and gets traded on. By the time strong data is published, whoever wanted to buy on that expectation has already bought. The confirmation lands — and there are no buyers left, but plenty of sellers: the very people who bought on the expectation and are now taking profit. Hence «buy the rumor, sell the fact» — not a strategy, not a conspiracy, just a description of a buyer flow running out.

Mechanism two — the surprise is smaller than expected

The data is strong, but weaker than consensus — and the market falls on «good» news because what's traded is the gap, not the level.

Mechanism three — repricing the consequences

Strong data means tighter central-bank policy (chapter 2), which is bad for risk assets. «Good news for the economy» and «good news for the asset» are different things, and often opposite ones.

What we're NOT saying. That «smart money deliberately sells into the crowd». No such coordinated mechanism exists; buyer-flow exhaustion and profit-taking are enough on their own to explain what's observed. The difference matters: the first explanation turns the user into a victim, the second into a market participant who can do math.

WHY A NUMBER EXACTLY ON FORECAST STILL MOVES THE MARKET

A counterintuitive block that fully closes chapter 5's cliffhanger. The surprise is zero, and there's still a move. Three reasons:

Uncertainty gets resolved. Before publication, market makers keep the book thin. Afterward, orders come back, and the return of liquidity itself moves price, regardless of the content.

Positions taken to hedge the uncertainty get closed. Hedges bought «just in case» are no longer needed.

The odds on other events get repriced. Inflation landing exactly on forecast means the central bank decision in two weeks just became more predictable — and that gets repriced instead.

The practical takeaway: volatility is caused by the resolution of uncertainty, not the news itself. That's why the compression before a release (Bollinger Bands, below) isn't a coincidence or «big players storing up energy» — it's a direct consequence of the book emptying out and then refilling.

THREE TYPES OF RELEASES

📊 Scheduled numbers

The date is known a year ahead, the hour a month ahead, the forecast a week ahead. NFP, CPI, retail sales, GDP. The value is the only unknown. This whole chapter is about these.

🏦 Central bank decisions

A bridge to chapter 2. The key difference: besides a number there's a text and a press conference, and the market often moves on the wording rather than the decision. The reaction stretches over hours, not minutes.

⚡ Unscheduled

The calendar is powerless here. The only defense isn't position size relative to a forecast — it's position size, period (chapter 3). We say it plainly: what this chapter teaches doesn't apply to these events.

CLIFFHANGER → CHAPTER 7

Now you know when the market will move, and you have a rough sense of how differently that can turn out, per our own measurements. And you know what the calendar doesn't tell you — which way.

But look at the scatter diagram one more time. On a large surprise, the move isn't always large — we measured that honestly. But at the moment of any surprise, someone is buying and selling enough volume to move the price within thirty minutes. Not a thousand retail traders on their phones. Someone — one participant, or a few.

You can't see them on a candlestick chart — a candle shows the outcome, not the participants. But they leave a trace, and it can be read: in where orders disappear, where they come back, and how price behaves in the twenty seconds when a large participant is building a position without wanting to move the market against themselves.
