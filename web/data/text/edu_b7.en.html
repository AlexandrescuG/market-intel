ANTI-MYTH

The myth here comes in layers, and they need peeling one at a time

First layer: «institutions don't exist, the market is everyone against everyone». Obviously wrong: the overwhelming share of turnover is banks, funds, treasuries and algorithms, not individuals.

Second layer, the main one in this chapter: «institutions hunt retail stops». That's disproved by arithmetic, not opinion. Retail volume is statistically invisible against interbank flow: coming specifically for it would be like robbing a bank for the coins in the tip jar by the door. Cascades on order clusters are real, though — Osler measured them across nine and a half thousand orders — but the clusters aren't made of retail stops, and they don't fire because of anyone's decision; they fire because orders are bunched together (chapter 5, §3.6).

Third layer, the stickiest one: «smart money leaves readable footprints on the candlestick chart, you just need to know the patterns». That's a testable claim. We tested it — below.

The truth, once all three layers are peeled back, is more useful than any of them: a large participant doesn't hide. They try not to move the price — and that very caution leaves a trace, visible in a few specific places, none of which is a shape on a chart.

THE PRICE OF NOT KNOWING

A rule you can't get out of

A trader learned that after liquidity gets swept and a gap stays unfilled, price gets «pulled» back. They see the setup on the chart, enter against the move, place a stop past the extreme.

What happens next comes in two versions. In the first, the setup works, and they lock in the rule. In the second, it doesn't, and they explain it as «I marked the zone wrong». Both versions confirm the original belief: the rule is right, the execution was wrong. That's a structure you can't get out of, because it isn't falsifiable.

The cost isn't in any single loss. It's that the person stops measuring: they have an explanation for every outcome, so there's no reason to run the statistics. We ran them instead — below — and the number isn't catastrophic, it's exactly the worst kind: close to random. A rule that works half the time doesn't blow up an account fast. It just slowly eats it through commissions and spread, leaving the feeling that «just a bit more and it'll click».

The honest second half: this doesn't mean patterns are useless. It means a pattern isn't a signal — it's a way to describe context, and the decision gets made by position size and risk management (chapter 3), not by the presence of a shape.

To understand the footprint, you have to understand the problem. It's the exact opposite of what usually gets told.

A large participant doesn't want to move the price

HOW EXECUTION ACTUALLY WORKS

Four cards. All documented and verifiable — no claims about intent.

🔪 Slicing

An order gets cut into dozens or hundreds of smaller pieces and stretched over time. The academic framework is the optimal-execution problem: a balance between the cost of immediacy and the risk that price drifts away while you're filling. It isn't a stealth tactic — it's the solution to an optimization problem.

🤖 Execution algorithms

VWAP — fill proportionally to the typical volume profile so the average fill matches the market's volume-weighted average. TWAP — evenly over time. POV — a fixed share of current volume. These algorithms are standard, sold by terminal vendors, and they're exactly why large moves often look like a series of matching steps rather than one lunge. Direct link to chapter 5: VWAP execution is tied to the hourly volume profile — a profile we measured ourselves.

🧊 Icebergs

An order where only part is visible; as it fills, the visible part replenishes. These exist as a standard order type on exchanges. Hence the observed effect: depth at a level «never runs out», no matter how much of it you eat.

📞 Off the book

A significant share of large size never executes in the open book at all: requests for quote from several dealers, internal netting, dark pools (chapter 11). The practical takeaway, unwelcome for the mythology: the largest trades are never visible in the book.

A separate note on COT

The only source where «institutional positions» aren't a metaphor — they're a published number. The CFTC publishes a weekly breakdown of open interest by participant category. The caveats have to sit right next to it: this is futures, not spot; the data is as of Tuesday, published Friday — so it lags; the «commercial» category isn't a synonym for «smart money», it's hedgers, and by construction their position is often against the trend. Caveats and all, this is an order of magnitude closer to reality than any candlestick pattern.

An honest caveat about spot forex

The market depth you see in your terminal for a currency pair is your liquidity provider's book, not the whole market's. Forex has no single order book: it's an OTC market, and every broker has its own flow. That doesn't invalidate the observation — book behavior, how fast density recovers, the reaction to a release are all visible in it too — but it matters to understand you're looking at a slice, not the whole. For futures and equities, the book is exchange-based and unified.

On the first run, double tops and bottoms showed noticeably higher agreement than every other pattern — around 70-77% instead of an honest 45-54%. The cause wasn't the pattern — it was us: fractal extremes only confirm a few candles after the peak, and the measurement started from the peak itself — a point that, in real time, wasn't known yet.
